Problem Hook & Value Proposition

Responses

  • Chosen path, which scenario are you committing to? (StreamLine or RouteLogic, or your own): StreamLine
  • Strategic crisis, based on the brief, if the company does nothing for 12 months, what happens to its market position?: If StreamLine does nothing, churn accelerates. Its advantage was volume, but the market has moved to curation, and more titles no longer win subscribers. The loyal, high-value viewers keep leaving for smaller specialized services, and within 12 months StreamLine slides from market leader to a commodity library competing on price instead of taste. The library keeps growing while its authority as a place for discovery disappears.
  • Moment of misery, what is the user likely doing outside the product (Google, spreadsheets, a competitor) because your tool isn’t serving them?: After scrolling for ten minutes without finding anything worth watching, the viewer gives up and looks elsewhere. They Google “best movies to watch,” check Reddit or ask a friend, or open a curated competitor like Mubi or Criterion that simply tells them what’s good. The decision of what to watch is happening outside StreamLine, which is the real failure.
  • Problem hook, summarize the business risk and user pain into one urgent sentence.: StreamLine’s library keeps growing while engagement flatlines, because viewers don’t need more titles, they need help finding the few worth watching, and every minute they spend lost in the catalog is a minute closer to canceling.
  • Value proposition, based on the proposed initiative (Spotlight or Velocity), what is the new value and why is it urgent to launch now?: Spotlight gives StreamLine’s most valuable viewers a curated, human-centered space that surfaces films worth their time instead of burying them in 15,000 titles. Less time searching, more time watching, and a real reason to stay. It’s urgent now because churn is already climbing and curated competitors are actively taking the high-value segment StreamLine can least afford to lose. Every quarter of delay hands them more of those viewers, and they do not come back.

Cold-read your own hook

  • Is the business risk high enough to justify a new initiative, a high-stakes threat, or a minor inconvenience?: Yes, high stakes. The loyal, high-value viewers are the hardest to replace and the most expensive to reacquire. Losing them to curated competitors drains both revenue and StreamLine’s reputation as a discovery destination, and that decline compounds. This is a threat to the core business, not a minor inconvenience.
  • Is the moment of misery a systemic problem or just an edge case?: Systemic. The problem is baked into the strategy. StreamLine’s answer to every gap has been to add more content, but more titles make discovery harder, not easier. Every viewer who values quality over volume hits the same wall. It’s structural.
  • Does the value proposition actually remove the obstacle you identified, or is it a generic feature?: It removes it. The pain is “I can’t find anything worth watching,” and curation attacks that directly rather than bolting on another generic feature. The one risk to stay honest about: the curation has to be genuinely good. A weak curated section is just one more row to scroll past.
1 · Module 3
Data-Backed Hypothesis

Finalized product hypothesis

Based on Aisha’s qualitative signal that discovery feels like a chore her segment opts out of, and the Snapshot 3 finding that Wanderers churn 22 points lower when exposed to Spotlight (the largest effect of any segment, across 41% of the base), I believe that removing the effort of discovery for Wanderers — surfacing curated content they’ll actually finish instead of a passive trending feed — will reduce their churn and flatten the Month 0→1 retention leak, as measured by a lift in curated-engagement rate among exposed Wanderers within 6 to 8 weeks and a sustained double-digit churn improvement over a matched control. I will protect Power User retention and session length as the guardrail, and I will make a go/no-go decision to scale once the Spotlight cohorts reach Month 5 to 6 and confirm the flatter retention curve holds.

Success metrics

Metric · Type · Target · Why it matters

Curated completion rate (Wanderers) · North-star · 18% → 35% of Wanderer sessions curated + finished · The behavior change the bet rests on; causes the retention, moves early

Weekly Wanderer sessions · Leading indicator · 1.1 → 1.6+ per week vs control (6–8 wks) · Earliest signal discovery stopped being a chore; your tripwire

Power User retention + session length · Guardrail · No drop >1 pt; stays 58%+ curated · Protects the valuable 22% you already retain from curation dilution

3 · Module 4
Roadmap, PRD & Prototype

Your strategic anchors

  • Persona (M2), who are you solving for?: The wonderer
  • Primary success metric (M3), your leading indicator: 20+ minutes watch
  • Moment of misery (M2), the specific friction blocking the goal: the moment it feels most broken

The instant she closes a 15,000-title app with nothing to watch and reaches for a disc. Maximum choice, zero payoff, and the product she pays for just lost to a shelf.

  • Guardrail metric (M3), what must not drop or break: Power User retention and session length must not drop. Power Users are 22% of the base, the highest LTV ($14.20), already low-churn, and already 58% curated. If reorienting Spotlight toward Wanderers dilutes the curation quality that retains Power Users, you win the cheap segment and bleed the valuable one. Protect their session length and curated-content satisfaction above all. Secondary guardrail: overall app session-completion rate, so a Wanderer-driven redesign doesn’t worsen the mid-session drop-off seen in Snapshot 1.

Audit, override & decide

  • Where did you override the AI? (feature + old vs. new score + why): The AI docked A4 because its core move (pick a mood before anything loads) asks the passive Wanderer to do work at the exact point they refuse to. That reasoning is sound for the pure persona. I overrode it because a single tap on login isn’t the same class of effort as a search or a scroll, and because mood-entry serves the adjacent “quiet Sunday” browser even if the hardest-core Wanderer skips it. My guardrail on the override: in the pilot, A4 is an optional enhancement layered on the default rail (A1), not a gate. If the Wanderer ignores the mood picker, the curated rail still loads. That way the feature can only add value, never reintroduce the friction the AI was worried about. If it were a mandatory step, I’d have left the AI’s score alone.
  • Did the AI over-value a Sales/Eng request your M2 interviews don’t support?: No, the opposite. It did exactly what ruthless scoring should: it gutted every non-research feature.

A8 Watch Party (Sales): Value 1, Time Sinker. No interview mentions social viewing, and the Wanderer is a solo passive viewer. Correctly killed.

A10 Offline Download (Sales): Value 2, Time Sinker. Serves someone who already chose a title. Not a discovery fix, no M2 support. Correct.

A9 Advanced Filter Engine (Engineering): Value 1, Time Sinker. This is the sharp one. More filters is what an engineer thinks discovery means, but it adds effort at the point of choice, which deepens the moment of misery for this persona. The AI scored it against the user, not the sponsor. Correct.

So the AI didn’t over-value any of them. If anything it was appropriately cruel, every Sales/Eng-origin feature landed in the kill zone, and the M2 interviews back that placement (no one asked for parties, downloads, or more filters; they asked for a human to hand them something good).

  • Did it underweight something your M3 cohort/funnel data strongly supports?: Yes. A6, the Spotlight Digest Email. The AI scored it Value 4 but filed it as a Fill-In, and I’d argue the M3 data supports a higher weight than its placement implies.

The cohort and segment data shows the Wanderer logs in 1.1 sessions a week. Every homepage feature, A1 through A4, only fires when the Wanderer actually opens the app. A6 is the only feature on the board that reaches a drifting Wanderer who isn’t logging in, which is precisely the behavior the M3 data says defines them. Tie it to the retention data: the Month 0→1 drop is the biggest leak in every cohort, and that leak is users quietly disengaging in their first weeks. An off-app re-engagement channel directly fights that leak in a way no homepage feature can. So the data arguably makes A6 a leading-value feature, not a nice-to-have. I’m holding it at Fill-In for the pilot because it’s a reasonable call, but the honest note is that the M3 data underwrites it more strongly than the quadrant shows, and it’s the first card I’d promote if the pilot signal comes in thin.

One thing the AI got right on the M3 side, worth stating: it correctly elevated features that serve the Wanderer’s passivity (A1 default rail) over features that serve curation enthusiasm (A7 curator-following), which matches the data showing the payoff concentrates in the low-curation Wanderer segment, not the already-curated Power User.

Generate your interactive roadmap

  • My “Now” lane (this sprint), the 2 to 3 quick wins I’ll build first: Four quick wins, all Effort 1-2, all aimed at one job: get a passive Wanderer to a curated title they’ll still be watching at minute 20.

A1 Spotlight Curated Rail — the core intervention. A default hand-picked rail that replaces the zero-payoff scroll with zero effort from the user. Everything else supports this.

A2 “Why You’ll Love This” Label — a reason at the point of choice, converting a browse into a press-play.

A6 Spotlight Digest Email — the only feature that reaches a Wanderer who logs in 1.1×/week, so it fights the churn that happens off-app.

A3 (Hidden Gem Badge) is the fourth, included because it’s nearly free. If forced to 3, A1 + A2 + A6 are the irreducible core: rail, reason, reach. The job of the sprint is build and instrument, not prove the metric, three weeks at 1.1 logins a week isn’t enough sessions to read the 20-minute rate cleanly.

  • What I cut, and the “no” I’m protecting the scope from: Cut A8 Watch Party, A9 Advanced Filter Engine, A10 Offline Download. Two came from Sales, one from Engineering, and none came from the user research. Each fails the Wanderer on both axes: wrong persona and too heavy for the window. A9 is the sharpest cut, more filters add effort at the exact point of choice, which deepens the moment of misery instead of removing it.

The “no” I’m protecting: no feature earns a slot because a stakeholder wants it; it earns a slot by moving a passive viewer’s 20-minute watch rate. That single rule is what keeps a 3-person, 3-week sprint from being hijacked by a sales demo or an engineer’s filter preference. I also deferred A5 (right feature, can’t build and measure an AI queue in 3 weeks) and A4 (asks the passive persona to act first), so “not now” is doing as much work as “no.”

Module 6

Individual Insights

Where the work was hardest, the moment my thinking changed, and what I learned.

Friction points

The baseline was the hardest single thing. I kept trying to state my primary metric’s current rate and realized the number wasn’t in my data, the closest figure was all-user and at the wrong threshold, and it probably overstated my actual segment. Working through it meant admitting the honest answer was “instrument it first,” and flagging 11% as a proxy rather than pretending it was fact. That one wrong cell cascaded into sample size, duration, and the split, so fixing it fixed four other things.

The second friction was separating the primary metric from the guardrail. I kept letting a session-length number leak into a metric that should have been a rate. They measure different things for different segments, and keeping them clean took real discipline

Aha moment

The persona realignment. I built the whole case around Priya, the loyal film buff who scrolls and finds nothing, and then the segment data said the payoff wasn’t with her at all. By the numbers she’s a Power User who already retains. The money was with the Wanderer, the low-engagement drifter I hadn’t even interviewed. The aha: the persona you design for is not always the segment that pays off, and when the data relocates the opportunity, you follow the data instead of marrying the vivid story. Priya stayed as the human face, but the ROI lived one segment over.

Key learnings

Constraints move the effort axis, not the value axis. When the timeline changed from three weeks to six months, the only feature that changed quadrant was the one that was worth building but blocked by time, the Sales and Engineering pet features stayed worthless, because their problem was never effort. Value is set by the persona, not the calendar.

“Quick win” and “must have” are different questions. The cheap, easy feature scored high on effort-value but was only a “could” on MoSCoW, because easy to build doesn’t mean necessary to ship. Running both frameworks on the same backlog showed me they rank things differently, and conflating them is how sprints fill with cheap optional work while the real must slips.

The dependency you don’t own can sink your metric. My 20-minute watch rate lived or died on a playback bug three teams away. Naming it as a blocking dependency was the one line that separated a real PRD from a wish, and the prototype proved it by mislabeling a buffering exit as a curation signal, exactly the confound I’d have missed on paper.
